Fafnir: The Gold Does Not Rest

By T.R. Pomeroy · 3 min read · November 25, 2026

A father’s hoard. A brother’s scheme. A dragon’s blood—and a warning in the trees.

A dramatic retelling by T.R. Pomeroy, drawn from the Fafnir and Sigurd story in Völsunga saga. Imagined motives, dialogue and sensory details are storytelling, not a literal translation or historical evidence. The episode’s saga sources are linked in the accompanying blog post.

If you could become a god… would you kill your father to do it? If you could hoard enough gold to never fear hunger again… would you leave your humanity behind? One ring. One curse. And one brother who said yes. Fafnir. Not yet a dragon. Not yet a myth. Just a son—strong, cunning, and firstborn to Hreidmar, who had just received the cursed hoard from Loki. It glittered beneath their halls like bottled sunlight. Gold, gems, and—at the center—Andvari’s Ring. The ring that multiplied treasure, cursed to multiply ruin. Loki had paid the blood price, and the gods had left.

But gold does not rest. It stirs. It whispers. And it began whispering to Fafnir. The curse worked quickly.

Hreidmar, intoxicated by his wealth, refused to share it. He stood over it like a king stands over his army—forgetting that his sons, Fafnir and Regin, were not soldiers… but wolves. Fafnir killed him in the night. Not with fire. With silence. With a blade between the ribs. And the moment the blood hit the stone floor, the gold began to change him.

He took the treasure to a desolate wasteland and built no palace, no throne. Instead, he buried himself in a cave, coiled around the treasure like a serpent—and over time, became one. Not metaphorically. He transformed. This is one of the oldest stories of shapeshifting greed: men, through the overwhelming strength of desire, transform. Fafnir loved gold so fiercely that he became the fiercest creature alive, a dragon, and he guarded the gold with all his might. Not through sorcery. Through lust. Through fear. Through the curse.

Back in the halls of men, Regin watched. He, too, had wanted the treasure—but lacked the strength to kill Fafnir. And so he waited. Schemed. And, in time, raised a boy. Sigurd. If you’ve never heard of him, imagine a Viking Hero. Like King Arthur or Hercules. Brave, honorable, doomed. Sigurd had never seen a dragon. But he had seen injustice. He listened to Regin’s stories of the stolen treasure, the monstrous brother, the rightful inheritance. And Regin, like all good manipulators, gave Sigurd what no one else would: A cause.

Sigurd agreed to kill Fafnir. Not for gold. Not for glory. For justice. What happens next is saga legend.

Sigurd digs a trench near the path Fafnir uses to slither toward water. When the dragon comes to drink, the boy waits beneath the soil—silent. When the shadow blocks out the sun, when the breath of the beast heats the earth… He strikes. Sword through belly. Fire in the sky. Blood like molten bronze pours over the boy. Fafnir dies. And with him, the last remnant of who he was: a brother. A son. A dwarf once driven by desperation. Now just bones and legend.

But the ring lives on. And this is where the curse sharpens its claws.

Because Regin was never honest. The gold was cursed. The dragon wasn’t a monster—he was a man. Regin planned to kill Sigurd next and take it all for himself.

But Sigurd had tasted the dragon’s blood while roasting its heart. He touched it, burned his finger, and put that finger to his mouth. The blood gave him something strange. The power to understand the language of birds. And the birds? They warned him. Regin’s betrayal. The danger of the ring. The gold’s curse. All whispered in leaves and wings and wind.

So Sigurd, the hero, the avenger of evil—beheaded his own foster-father. Not for gold. But for truth.

And then… he took the treasure anyway. Because in the end, the curse always wins.

About this excerpt

From the manuscript Ep. 006 The Cursed Ring, by T.R. Pomeroy.

Selected narrative passages, with spoken line breaks joined for reading. Hreidmar is named rather than called a dwarf king. Regin is identified as Sigurd’s foster-father, and the blood-tasting scene follows chapter 19: Sigurd is roasting the heart when he burns his finger and tastes its blood. A line implying that the gold was not stolen and an unsupported confession by Regin have been corrected. Dramatic imagery and motives remain the author’s retelling.
