Solveig at the Chieftain’s Door

By T.R. Pomeroy · 4 min read · November 24, 2026

Winter closes in on an outlaw’s family. Solveig refuses to let silence decide their fate.

After her brother Aksel’s outlawry, Solveig has been turned away at the market. Her family faces winter with dwindling food. These are fictional characters; their treatment is part of the story, not a universal account of Norse law.

Inside the longhouse, her mother counted stores again. Dried herbs hung from the rafters. Barrels of barley stacked in the corner of their small house. The look on her mother’s face suggested they had just enough to make it through the winter, to keep the sheep fed and then plant a fresh crop in spring. Einar said little, most of the time he was distant. It had taken many years for him to come to terms with his injury, but losing his son created a wound deeper than any sword could make.

Solveig returned home from the market with nothing to show for it. Not even a scrap of dried fish. She laid the folded blanket back on the bench beside the hearth. The mittens beside it. The tunics she had stitched by lamplight last winter. Work that once meant security now felt like a question with no answer. Her mother did not scold her. She only nodded once — slow, quiet — and went back to counting and calculating how long it would all last.

Outside, the wind had shifted. It no longer brushed the walls. It pressed against them. That night the first real snow came — not a dusting, not polite frost — but heavy, wet, and relentless. By morning the path to the well had vanished. The sheep huddled close in their pen. The hens refused to leave the coop. Einar stood in the doorway, his useless arm hanging stiff at his side, watching the sky like a man who had once read waves for danger, but now read the clouds and the expression on his wife’s face instead.

But it was her face that sent a chill down his spine. She had the look of a ship captain who knew the approaching storm might very well sink the whole crew, ship and all. The next few weeks were cold, and the darkness lingered longer in the mornings and came too quick in the evening. Solveig could hear her parents speaking in hushed tones in the darkness when they thought she was asleep, but who can sleep when your stomach is empty?

Her parents debated what they could do. Journey to the next village on foot, rob the neighbors’ cellar for a few more turnips. They knew they didn’t have enough to make it through the winter. Even if spring came early, it wouldn’t be early enough. Einar had waited as long as he could. After several days without fresh snow — a narrow window carved out by mercy or chance — he decided.

He would go. He rose before dawn, without lighting the hearth. The hall was dim, the embers low. He dressed slowly, carefully, pulling on the old cloak he had worn in younger years. He gathered the wool goods Solveig had failed to trade — the blanket, the tunics, the mittens — and slid the knife at his belt, the one that had served him in the fields and in battle.

Solveig lay still, watching through half-closed eyes. She heard him whisper to her mother. “I will go south,” he said. “I’ll see what I can trade… even the blade, if I must.” Her mother’s voice broke in the darkness. “You won’t make it.” Einar did not answer. When the door opened, the cold rushed in like a verdict. It filled the hall, sharp and final, reminding them all that doing nothing meant certain death.

Solveig understood. But beneath the fear was something hotter — the injustice of it. A community that had turned its back on them because of their blood. Because of her brother. An outlaw condemned too quickly, judged too harshly, and now spoken of like a curse. Winter was not their only enemy. Silence was. Moments after her father disappeared into the pale gray dawn, Solveig rose. She dressed quickly, jaw set, and slipped out the door without a word.

Her mother watched her go. Solveig walked with purpose toward the village. It was still early. Smoke had not yet risen from most roofs. Frost lay untouched on the path. She stopped before the longhouse of the chieftain — the largest in the settlement. Inside slept the chieftain, his sons, their wives, and servants. What she did not know was that another elder had joined them, their own roof having collapsed in the last heavy snow.

Solveig stepped forward. She pounded on the door. Kicked it. Shouted into the wood until her knuckles burned. The hall stirred. Voices rose inside. And as the door latch began to lift, Solveig drew a breath and spoke the words that would either save her family… or seal their fate as outcasts forever.
