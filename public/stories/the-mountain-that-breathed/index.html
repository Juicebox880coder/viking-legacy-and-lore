The Mountain That Breathed

By T.R. Pomeroy · 4 min read · November 24, 2026

By the longhouse fire, Haesten tells of a crack in the mountain—and what waited inside.

Haesten gathers the children around the hearth to tell Olvar’s tale. These characters and this scene belong to the author’s storytelling; this is not presented as an attested medieval Troll Queen saga.

The longhouse breathed like a living beast. Its thick timber ribs curved overhead, blackened by decades of smoke. Firelight licked the beams, casting shadows that swayed like spirits along the walls. Outside, the winter wind clawed at the turf roof, moaning through the cracks… but inside—inside was warm. Alive. And ancient. The air smelled of pine pitch and roasting mutton, of wool cloaks damp from snow, of smoke so thick you could almost taste it on your tongue. Children huddled on fur pelts near the central hearth, knees pulled tight to their chests, eyes wide with the kind of expectation only children still know how to feel. Their cheeks glowed red from heat and excitement.

And there, on the low wooden stool carved with knotwork spirals worn smooth by years of hands, sat Haesten. White beard braided. Weathered skin like cured leather. One eye milky from age, the other sharp as a raven’s—always watching. Always measuring who was ready for a story… He was loved by children. And reluctantly tolerated by adults. Because Haesten told the old tales. The ones adults pretended they’d outgrown. The ones children instinctively understood were still true.

He leaned forward, his joints popping like damp wood in the fire. “Gather close,” he rasped. “Tonight, I tell you a tale my grandfather heard from a trapper up in the mountains that lie beyond the fjord.” “A man who climbed where no animal even dared to venture.” “This was a man who learned—too late—that not every stone stays asleep.” A few adults in the longhouse chuckled under their breath.

Haesten ignored them. He always did.

Haesten let the silence linger—not the silence of boredom, but the heavy, listening kind. The kind that makes even grown men straighten up to hear what’s next. “The trapper’s name,” Haesten said, “was Olvar. A quiet man. Lived alone. Trusted more in snares and snow than he did in people.” “One morning, Olvar climbed the ridge above the valley—higher than he’d ever gone. The air was thin as a knife-edge. The snow crunched beneath his boots. And then…”

Haesten paused. The fire popped once—sharp as a warning. A draft slid beneath the door, making the flames bend as if listening. “…he noticed something odd. A crack. A long, black split in the stone. And the stone around it…” Haesten lowered his voice until the children leaned closer, drawn in by the suspense. “It was warm.” “Olvar felt wind blow from that crack. Only it wasn’t wind.”

Haesten put his hand on his chest. “It was breath.” “And Olvar realized,” Haesten whispered, “that he was not alone.” The longhouse seemed to hold its collective breath. “And that crack…” He pointed his stick directly at a wide-eyed little boy near the front. “…was starting… to widen.” “Olvar knelt,” Haesten continued, “and pressed his palm against the rock. It was warm. Not from the sun. Not fire. But from what felt like warm air from the crack.”

“A sound came from inside the crack. Slow. Deep. Like stone shifting its weight. The crack grew large enough for Olvar to see movement inside. A shape, a figure.”

“Free me.” “Olvar ran,” Haesten said. “He ran because he understood something the moment that voice spoke.” “That whatever slept beneath the mountain was awake, and it was not kind.” Haesten stopped. Let the moment hover. Let the dread settle. Let every listener feel the cold air creeping in around their ankles. Then he leaned back, exhaling through his beard like a tired old wolf that lay down to rest.

And here—right here—is where the room changed. Because this wasn’t just a campfire story. Not just an old man’s tale. Not just a troll fable meant to scare children into staying close to home. No—this was the moment where every person by that fire, child and adult, felt the same cold truth rising in their chests: Some boundaries are crossed long before we realize we stepped over them.

Some cracks widen whether we are ready or not. And evil refused to stay buried.
