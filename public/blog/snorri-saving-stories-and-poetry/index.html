Snorri: Saving Stories and Poetry

By T.R. Pomeroy · 2 min read · November 24, 2026

The Prose Edda preserves more than myths. It also teaches the language of poetry.

Snorri was born in 1179 in Iceland, long after the Viking Age had begun to fade. By the time he was growing up, most people in the North were Christian, and the old Norse gods like Odin and Thor were no longer being worshipped the way they once had been. But something interesting was happening. The stories were still around. Poets still told tales about Odin, Thor, Loki, and the end of the world called Ragnarök.

Snorri was not just a farmer or a warrior. He was well educated. He studied law, language, poetry, and history. He became very skilled in each. Later in life he became one of the most powerful leaders in Iceland. He served as the Lawspeaker. Snorri was smart, wealthy, well connected and very interested in stories.

The language of the Viking age was still being used. But the meaning was slowly disappearing. Snorri understood the importance of writing these stories down. So Snorri decided to do something bold. He would write a book that explained the old Norse myths and the poetry connected to them. Around the year 1220, he wrote a book we now call the Prose Edda.

Its three main parts followed a prologue. The first main section focused on the story of the Norse universe. It explained how the world was created, who the gods were, and how the world would end at Ragnarok.

Second was a section which explained the special language used by poets. And third was the part which taught the rules and rhythms of poetry. Here he’s teaching less about myth, and more about mastery. Snorri isn’t just preserving stories—he’s teaching people how to speak like the past. Keep in mind Snorri was not trying to bring back the old religion. He was trying to save the stories and the poetry.

Historical context & sources

Snorri wrote in Christian medieval Iceland, after the conventional Viking Age. The Prose Edda is a major witness to Norse mythology, but not the work of its only preserver or an unfiltered record of a single earlier religion. Its three principal sections follow a prologue. [1]
