Silver: A Language of Trade

By T.R. Pomeroy · 2 min read · November 24, 2026

Cut, weighed, divided and shared—silver could cross boundaries that swords could not.

Silver could be counted, measured, divided, and shared. A warrior might earn a silver arm ring for his bravery, a mark of honor visible to all. A farmer might save hacked pieces under his floorboards, hoping to trade for cattle or a new blade come spring. And a chieftain? He might wear it layered, heavy, and obvious—because silver didn’t whisper status. It shouted it.

It wasn’t just wealth. It was a language. And the Vikings spoke it fluently. But there’s another reason silver held such power—one more practical, more immediate, and far more revealing. And to understand that… We have to step into the marketplace. Because silver could go where swords could not.

Across borders, across languages, across loyalties, the Vikings needed something that could move as quickly as they did. Silver was that something. It didn’t matter if the piece came from Baghdad, Byzantium, or Britain. As long as it weighed true, it could be used. Coins were clipped and cut, bracelets chopped into pieces—hacksilver—then weighed on small balance scales carried in pouches like weapons of trade.

It was immediate. Tangible. Trusted. And it allowed the Norse to build a vast network of raiding, trading, and tribute-taking without relying on any foreign kingdom’s rules. They could sail into a port, sell furs or slaves, buy grain or weapons, and be gone before the next tide. Silver was their key—to markets, to alliances, to independence. It wasn’t bound by borders.

And it wasn’t just about convenience. Because beyond the weight and the shine, silver carried something far more powerful— meaning. And that… changed everything. Silver wasn’t just metal to the Vikings—it was identity.
