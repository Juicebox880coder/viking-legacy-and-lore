The Wolfskin: The Cost of Becoming

By T.R. Pomeroy · 2 min read · November 26, 2026

Sigmund, Sinfjötli, and a question the wolfskin leaves behind: what does strength cost if you lose yourself?

The Völsunga saga tells of two men—Sigmund and his son Sinfjötli—who discover two enchanted wolf pelts hanging in a house in the forest. When they wear them, they take on the behavior of wolves. They howl, understand one another, and cannot remove the skins until the appointed time. And during that time… they attacked even those they loved. Once they can take the skins off, they burn them. For me, it isn’t only a story about shapeshifting. It’s a warning about what happens when you become the thing you hate.

Now ask yourself—how many times can you pretend before it stops being pretending? How many raids does it take before the bloodlust doesn’t fade? At what point do you stop wearing the wolfskin… and start being it? That’s what makes this story of werewolves so terrifying. It isn’t only magic. It’s identity.

You wear the skin to fight better. To survive. To protect your people. But in doing so… you risk forgetting what it feels like to be a person at all.

The werewolf wasn’t just a creature in the woods. He was a man who no longer wanted to be one. The wolfskin gave strength. A new identity. And the feeling of overwhelming power and freedom from human limitations. But the cost? Your name. Your memory. Your soul. And maybe even the ones you love. Because once you become the beast to win the fight… It doesn’t always come off.

Don’t glamorize the beast. Don’t wear the skin the world tries to hand you. And whatever you do— Don’t lie to yourself and say you can take it off whenever you want. The real war isn’t out there. It’s within. And it won’t be won by rage, denial, or strength. It’ll be won by the one who has the courage to face it— without becoming it.

So when it rises in you— That need to lash out. To dominate. To be something else. To feed the wolf… You have to ask: Is it worth it? To gain that thing you desire and lose yourself in the end?

Historical context & sources

The opening draws on chapter 8 of Völsunga saga, a medieval legendary narrative, not evidence that people physically transformed into wolves. The reading of the wolfskin as a warning about identity is T.R. Pomeroy’s interpretation. The passage does not establish a universal Viking belief or a documented warrior ritual. [1]

  1. Völsunga saga, chapter 8 — William Morris Archive, University of Iowa

    Sigmund and Sinfjötli put on enchanted wolfskins, howl with mutual understanding, and later burn the skins after removing them. English translation by William Morris and Eiríkr Magnússon (1870); linked for comparison, not reproduced here.
